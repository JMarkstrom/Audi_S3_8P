ZF electric power steering maps compared

Unlike earlier generations of Audi, Volkswagen, Škoda and other VAG vehicles, where steering characteristics were determined primarily by the mechanical hardware, PQ35-platform vehicles equipped with the third-generation ZF electric power steering system can have their steering feel changed through software. This page compares different ZF .sgo parameter sets used by the electro-mechanical steering systems across the VAG 8P/Mk6 platform. The aim is to show how the calibrations differ and, more importantly, what those differences may mean for the way the car feels to drive.

Why these maps differ, and what this page is not

Not every car supports changing the steering software, and the available software path is not necessarily the same between models. In addition, some differences between calibrations are there to compensate for the characteristics of the vehicle itself — including its layout, 2WD versus AWD configuration, and engine weight. The five-cylinder engine used in the Audi RS models, for example, is considerably heavier than the four-cylinder engines found in many of the other cars.

The comparisons that follow therefore should not be interpreted simply as a ranking of the calibrations. They are intended to show the characteristics of each calibration and help owners understand what changes may be achievable on their own car.

Verify the file before you use it. This page does not supply the .sgo files. Source the file you want yourself, compute its SHA-256, and compare that with the digest on the card above. A match means you have the same bytes that were decoded here. A mismatch means you have a different file.

The curve file may not be enough. Depending on the software already in the steering rack, the rack itself must be upgraded before a curve can be flashed.

Note: The numbers on this page were read from the files and cross-checked. What they mean was not: these compiled objects have no parameter names, units or scale factors. Axis labels such as speed or assist are inferences from the data, so treat the names as hypotheses and the values as solid.

The assist ladder is the one inference checked on the road: maps 1–5 ordered by falling assist match how the TT RS, S3 and RS3 feel. The secondary speed-indexed maps predict the opposite and do not match, so their role is still unknown.

The Steering-Assist Ladder

The six largest records are 8-breakpoint curves stored back to back. Maps 1–5 form a graduated assist ladder, with map 1 providing the most assist and map 5 the least. Map 6 breaks this pattern and is byte-identical across all seven files, so it is not part of the speed ladder.

This is the main structure that distinguishes the calibrations from the driver's seat. The map 1–5 ordering is also supported by on-road impressions: the calibration with the most map-1 assist and least map-5 assist feels lightest when parking and firmest at speed.

Comparing Steering Effort

Because maps 1 to 5 are ordered by falling assist, the ladder can be collapsed into a single line per car. Drag the slider to pick an operating point. Effort mode asks how much steering torque each map needs to reach a fixed assist output, which rises across the ladder like a conventional effort-versus-speed plot. Assist mode asks how much assist each map delivers at a fixed input torque, which falls.

The operating point matters. Keep it in the everyday part of the curves, which is where the ordering is meaningful. Push the effort target above roughly 800 counts and the answer gets dominated by the extreme top of each curve, which inverts the ranking in a way that has nothing to do with normal driving.

Speed-indexed maps

These four maps are the only ones in the file with an unambiguous input axis: single-byte breakpoints running 0 to 250, which reads directly as vehicle speed in km/h (250 km/h is the platform limiter). The speed axis here is drawn to scale and shows each car's real breakpoints. These are secondary scalers: they do not track how the cars actually feel, so treat them as supporting detail rather than the main event.

Torque-indexed falloff maps

Two maps share the assist family's input axis, both starting at a power of two - 128 and 1024 - and decaying from there. That makes them fixed-point multipliers with 1.0 at zero input, so they are shown normalised to their own starting value.

The same two constants also appear as standalone scalars at 0x05E514 and 0x05E1D4, though VW Golf GTI and VW Golf R set the first of those to 192 while the map still starts at 128, so the pairing is suggestive rather than certain.

The ten-point map with a geometric input axis

Record 0x05E490 is the only 10-breakpoint curve. Its input steps grow by roughly 1.7x out to 13714 while the output stays close to linear, an axis shaped for a quantity with a very wide dynamic range. All seven cars share the same endpoints, 0 and 13714 in, 0 and 1400 out, and differ only in the middle.

Unlike the assist ladder, this curve does not appear to directly determine the speed-dependent steering weight described above; its exact role therefore remains uncertain.
